Autonomous Patrol Robot Systems Analysis

A systems analysis study evaluating U.S. market-entry feasibility for DOGU Robotics, a Korean autonomous security robot company. Developed a Robot Feasibility Index from 113 survey responses and 20 stakeholder interviews. Finished 2nd in the K-Capstone competition.

Python / Pandas MATLAB Survey Analysis Feasibility Modeling

Overview

A five person interdisciplinary team was assigned to evaluate DOGU Robotics' U.S. market entry across three deployment environments: indoor campus, outdoor campus, and hospital. The deliverable was a 10-minute final presentation with a market entry strategy recommendation.

The team developed a Robot Feasibility Index (RFI) informed by UTAUT and SERVQUAL frameworks, scoring each deployment environment across five factors: Need, Demand, Acceptance, Concern, and Gate. Need, Demand, Acceptance, and Concern were calculated separately from student survey data and expert interview data, producing distinct NDAC_student and NDAC_expert inputs before weighting. Gate used expert interview results only because it represented deployment feasibility from the operating stakeholder side. The analysis drew from 113 student survey responses and approximately 20 stakeholder interviews including UWPD, UW facilities staff, healthcare workers, and administrators.

Problem & Approach

The core challenge was translating broad stakeholder concerns into a quantified decision framework. Early work focused on market research and competitor benchmarking, but after client feedback the direction shifted toward a structured feasibility index.

The RFI framework scores each deployment environment on five factors. For Need, Demand, Acceptance, and Concern, student and expert data were first processed as separate NDAC_student and NDAC_expert score sets. Each factor then used a weighted combination of the student and expert values according to its importance in the overall decision model. Gate was treated differently: because it measures whether the operating organization could realistically deploy and support the robot, only expert interview results were used for that factor. The final RFI aggregates those weighted NDAC factors and the expert-based Gate score into one feasibility percentage per scenario. Competitor benchmarking compared DOGU's IROI and Patrover models against Knightscope K5 and SMP Robotics Argus across patrol duration, weather resistance, sensor configuration, autonomous docking, and pricing.

My Role

Within the five person team, my contributions spanned the analytical framework and software pipeline.

  • Drove the shift from broad market research toward the quantified RFI approach.
  • Proposed and developed the NDAC factor structure connecting Need, Demand, Acceptance, and Concern.
  • Drafted the initial Python and pandas analysis workflow for survey preprocessing and weighted scoring.
  • Connected UTAUT and SERVQUAL frameworks to the RFI factor definitions and interpretation logic.

A CSE teammate revised and finalized the analysis code. An IE teammate conducted the hospital analysis in R. Stakeholder interviews and student surveys were conducted at the team level.

Outcome

The RFI produced comparable scores for both campus environments, with hospital scoring highest. Student survey results showed strong baseline support but significant concern around overreliance and privacy.

Limitations

The most significant issue was a scope misalignment between the team's initial approach and the client's actual goals. The team originally aimed at a product-level competitive analysis, but DOGU Robotics wanted a broad market feasibility study. The technical benchmarking work from the early competitor analysis phase was not carried into the final framework.

Because the student survey and expert interviews were collected before the RFI framework was finalized, not every question mapped cleanly onto the five factors. Only compatible student and expert inputs were extracted and scored, so the RFI reflects a structured weighting of available data rather than a purpose-built instrument.

The hospital analysis was conducted by a teammate using R. I was not directly involved in that calculation and cannot fully verify the hospital scoring assumptions. The hospital RFI result of 50.4% formed the basis for the team's final market entry recommendation.

Reflection

This was my first direct experience working with an industry client on a real market entry problem. The most useful lesson was understanding how commercial deployment decisions are actually made — not purely through technical analysis, but through stakeholder needs, operational constraints, and user acceptance.

The scope misalignment early in the project was a clear lesson in requirements clarification. In a professional context, misreading the client's actual question costs more time than asking for clarification upfront would have. The RFI framework itself was a useful exercise in translating qualitative stakeholder concerns into a structured, comparable score — a skill that transfers directly to engineering trade studies and design review processes.
