High Power Rocket — NAR Level 1 Certification

My first hands on aerospace project. Built, prepped, and flew a high power rocket to earn NAR Level 1 certification. The certification was the direct foundation for the IREC build and the DBF aircraft work that followed.

High-Power Rocketry Stability Analysis Recovery Systems Composite Fabrication

Overview

NAR Level 1 is the entry point for high power rocketry: a certified flight on an H or I class motor with a rocket you have built and verified yourself. Before this project, I had no practical aerospace experience outside of A&P maintenance coursework. This was where the gap between understanding aircraft systems and actually building something that flies started to close.

Problem & Approach

NAR L1 certification requires a stable flight and a successful recovery on an H or I class motor. The two critical requirements are stability margin and recovery system reliability. If either fails, the certification attempt does not count.

Stability was verified physically: balancing the rocket to locate the center of gravity, then confirming that CG sat sufficiently forward of the estimated center of pressure. The accepted rule of thumb is at least one caliber of margin between CG and CP. Recovery used a single parachute deployment timed by an altimeter triggered ejection charge.

My Role

This was a solo certification flight. My contributions covered the full build and launch cycle:

  • Airframe assembly and epoxy bonding of motor mount, fins, and recovery bay.
  • Physical CG and CP stability verification using the finger balance method to confirm static margin before flight.
  • Recovery system rigging: parachute packing, shock cord attachment, and ejection charge sizing.
  • Pre-launch readiness at the White Swan, WA launch site.
  • Post-flight recovery of the vehicle.

Evidence

Launch footage from the certification flight at White Swan, WA.

Outcome

The flight was nominal: good apogee and a clean recovery. NAR Level 1 certification was earned on this flight.

No post-flight telemetry or altitude data was recorded. The certification is based on the observed flight and recovery, not on measured performance numbers.

Reflection

This project established the practical baseline I needed to take on more complex rocketry work. Understanding stability margin physically, not just as a simulation output, made the OpenRocket modeling I did for the IREC vehicle more grounded in what the numbers actually represent. Knowing what a properly rigged recovery system feels like in practice carried directly into the dual deploy setup on the IREC rocket.

The progression from this certification flight to the IREC build, and then to leading the DBF aircraft subteam, was not accidental. Each project exposed a specific gap the previous one could not address: L1 gave me the stability and recovery fundamentals; IREC put those under simulation and fabrication pressure; DBF pushed the design scope from rockets to fixed wing aerodynamics.
